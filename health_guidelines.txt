WHO & EPA Air Quality Health Guidelines — AirGuard Knowledge Base
==================================================================

AQI 0–50 (Good)
Air quality is considered satisfactory, and air pollution poses little or no risk.
No health precautions are needed for the general public. Ideal for all outdoor activities.

AQI 51–100 (Moderate)
Air quality is acceptable; however, for some pollutants there may be a moderate health
concern for a very small number of people who are unusually sensitive to air pollution.
Active children and adults, and people with respiratory disease (asthma), should limit
prolonged outdoor exertion.

AQI 101–150 (Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups)
Members of sensitive groups may experience health effects. The general public is unlikely
to be affected. Sensitive groups include: children, older adults, people with heart or
lung disease, and people with diabetes. Sensitive individuals should reduce prolonged or
heavy outdoor exertion.

AQI 151–200 (Unhealthy)
Everyone may begin to experience health effects. Members of sensitive groups may experience
more serious health effects. Active children and adults, and people with respiratory disease
(asthma), should avoid prolonged outdoor exertion. Everyone else, especially children,
should limit prolonged outdoor exertion.

AQI 201–300 (Very Unhealthy)
Health alert: everyone may experience more serious health effects. Active children and
adults, and people with respiratory disease (asthma), should avoid all outdoor exertion.
Everyone else, especially children, should limit outdoor exertion.

AQI 301+ (Hazardous)
Health warnings of emergency conditions. The entire population is more likely to be affected.
Everyone should avoid all outdoor exertion. People with heart or lung disease, older adults,
and children should remain indoors and keep activity levels low.

PM2.5 Health Effects
Fine particles (PM2.5) are 2.5 micrometres or smaller in diameter. They can penetrate deep
into the lungs and even enter the bloodstream, causing cardiovascular and respiratory harm.
Short-term exposure can cause eye, nose, throat and lung irritation, coughing, sneezing, and
shortness of breath. Long-term exposure is linked to heart attacks, reduced lung function,
lung cancer, and premature death.
WHO guideline: PM2.5 annual mean ≤ 5 µg/m³; 24-hour mean ≤ 15 µg/m³.

PM10 Health Effects
Particles smaller than 10 micrometres (PM10) include dust, pollen, and mould spores.
They can irritate the eyes, nose and throat. People with asthma, cardiovascular disease or
other respiratory conditions are most vulnerable. WHO guideline: PM10 annual mean ≤ 15 µg/m³.

Nitrogen Dioxide (NO₂) Health Effects
NO₂ is primarily a product of combustion from motor vehicles and power plants.
Short-term exposures can aggravate respiratory diseases, particularly asthma, leading to
respiratory symptoms such as coughing, wheezing, or breathing difficulty.
WHO guideline: annual mean ≤ 10 µg/m³; 24-hour mean ≤ 25 µg/m³.

Ozone (O₃) Health Effects
Ground-level ozone is created by chemical reactions between NOx and VOCs in sunlight.
Ozone can cause chest pain, coughing, throat irritation, and airway inflammation.
It can reduce lung function and worsen bronchitis, emphysema, and asthma.
Levels are typically highest on hot, sunny afternoons.
WHO guideline: 8-hour mean ≤ 100 µg/m³.

Sulfur Dioxide (SO₂) Health Effects
SO₂ is produced by burning fossil fuels and volcanic activity. Short-term exposure causes
breathing problems, especially in people with asthma. Long-term exposure leads to
respiratory illness and heart disease.
WHO guideline: 24-hour mean ≤ 40 µg/m³.

Carbon Monoxide (CO) Health Effects
CO is produced by incomplete combustion of carbon-based fuels (cars, generators, stoves).
At high concentrations it prevents oxygen from being absorbed by the body, causing headaches,
dizziness, weakness, and at very high levels, death. Ensure good ventilation when using
gas appliances or generators indoors.

Mask Recommendations
N95 or KN95 respirators filter ≥ 95% of airborne particles when worn correctly.
They are effective against PM2.5 and most biological particles.
Standard surgical masks offer limited protection against fine particles.
For AQI above 150, N95/KN95 masks are recommended for outdoor activities.
For AQI above 200, avoid outdoor activities even with a mask if possible.

Indoor Air Quality Tips
Keep windows closed during high pollution periods. Use HEPA air purifiers — look for a CADR
(Clean Air Delivery Rate) appropriate for your room size. Change air purifier filters
regularly (typically every 6-12 months). Avoid indoor combustion sources (incense, candles,
gas stoves without ventilation). Vacuum with a HEPA-filter vacuum cleaner.

Exercise and Air Quality
Exercising outdoors during poor air quality significantly increases pollutant intake because
breathing rate and depth increase. On days with AQI above 100, sensitive individuals should
exercise indoors. On days above 150, everyone should avoid vigorous outdoor exercise.
Morning hours (before 10 AM) and evenings (after 6 PM) often have lower ozone levels.

Diet and Air Quality
Antioxidant-rich foods help protect the body from oxidative stress caused by air pollutants.
Recommended foods: berries (blueberries, strawberries), leafy greens (spinach, kale),
nuts and seeds, omega-3 fatty acids (fish, flaxseed), green tea.
Vitamin C, Vitamin E, and beta-carotene are particularly protective against PM2.5 damage.

Children and Air Pollution
Children breathe more air relative to their body weight than adults and spend more time
outdoors. Their developing lungs and immune systems are more vulnerable. Air pollution
is linked to slower lung development, increased asthma risk, and lower cognitive performance
in children. Schools should monitor AQI and limit outdoor recess when AQI exceeds 100.

Elderly and Air Pollution
Older adults have decreased lung capacity and often have pre-existing cardiovascular or
respiratory conditions. They are disproportionately affected by PM2.5 and O₃ exposure.
Elderly individuals should check AQI daily and stay indoors on unhealthy air days.
